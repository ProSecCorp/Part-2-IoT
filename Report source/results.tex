\section{Results and discussion}
\label{sec:results}
Note that every result presented in this section is based on the \glspl{ids} output generated by the Python scripts, which are available in \texttt{Source/Results}.

In all experiments, \texttt{IDS\_sinkhole.py} reported at least one \texttt{[ALERT] suspicious rank decrease} for each simulated node.
This behavior was also observed in the baseline scenario and is therefore not sufficient, by itself, to identify a Sinkhole attack.

The false positives are mainly caused by the \gls{dodag} formation process.
Nodes initially have \texttt{RPL\_INFINITE\_RANK} and acquire a finite Rank when they receive the first valid DIO.
The \gls{ids} currently interprets this transition as a suspicious Rank decrease.
Therefore, Rank-decrease detection is considered too sensitive when applied during the initial \gls{dodag} construction and must be interpreted together with the Rank consistency check.

\subsection{Blackhole attack}
\label{subsec:blackhole_attack_results}
An example of a packet drop caused by a Blackhole attack is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:blackhole1_logs}.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{Screenshots/Blackhole1-DROP-example.PNG}
    \caption{Mote logs showing a packet drop caused by a Blackhole attack (scenario 1).}
    \label{fig:blackhole1_logs}
\end{figure}

We observed that the \gls{pdr} is significantly affected by the Blackhole attack, as expected, since the attacker drops all the packets it receives for forwarding.
The \gls{ids} correctly detected the Blackhole attack in scenarios 1 and 3, while it failed to detect the attack in scenario 2, where the attacker was a leaf node.
In this case, the attacker did not have any children and therefore did not receive packets requiring forwarding.
Consequently, the forwarding-based detection mechanism had no sufficient evidence to identify the malicious behavior.
Nevertheless, the significant reduction in \gls{pdr} would have provided a clear indication of the attack if this metric had also been monitored by the \gls{ids}.

The results hence show that the position of the attacker has a significant impact on the effectiveness of the attack.
In scenario 1, the attacker was located in the middle of the topology, causing only part of the traffic to be dropped and resulting in a \gls{pdr} of approximately $60\%$.
In scenario 3, the attacker was positioned at a bottleneck, causing all traffic to pass through it and resulting in a \gls{pdr} of $0\%$.

These results confirm that a Blackhole attack is strongly dependent on the attacker's position within the routing topology.
A node that is not part of any forwarding path may have no observable impact on network performance.

In scenario 4, the Blackhole attack was combined with a Sinkhole attack.
The Sinkhole component was correctly identified by the \gls{ids}, while the Blackhole behavior was not detected.
In particular, the forwarding ratio of the suspected node remained above the detection threshold, with a value of approximately $1.09$.
Therefore, the forwarding-based detection rule did not classify the node as malicious, despite the presence of the Blackhole behavior.
Again, this result highlights the importance of monitoring the \gls{pdr} as an additional metric to detect Blackhole attacks, especially when they are combined with other attack types.

\subsection{Sinkhole attack}
\label{subsec:sinkhole_attack_results}
An example of a Rank inconsistency caused by a Sinkhole attack is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:sinkhole2_logs}.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{Screenshots/Sinkhole2-FAKERANK-example.PNG}
    \caption{Mote logs showing a Rank inconsistency caused by a Sinkhole attack (scenario 2).}
    \label{fig:sinkhole2_logs}
\end{figure}

The Sinkhole scenarios produced a different behavior compared to the Blackhole experiments.
As expected, the attack did not directly affect packet delivery: the \gls{pdr} and throughput remained approximately unchanged across all Sinkhole scenarios.
The main effect of the attack was instead observed in the routing topology, as the malicious node attempted to attract neighboring nodes by advertising an artificially favorable Rank.

The reconstructed \glspl{dag} before and after the attack are shown for each scenario in order to highlight the changes introduced in the routing topology.
Together with the topology changes, the \gls{ids} was able to identify Rank inconsistencies involving the malicious node.

In the first three scenarios, the Sinkhole was placed at different positions in the topology.
In the bottleneck configuration, where the malicious node was a direct child of the root, the attack was detected because the malicious node advertised \texttt{ROOT\_RANK} as its Rank.
This resulted in an explicit Rank inconsistency between the malicious node and its parent.

However, the effectiveness of the attack also depends on how the advertised Rank is used by the \gls{mrhof} parent-selection mechanism.
In the original implementation, the path cost of a neighbor is computed from both its advertised Rank and its link metric:

\begin{equation}
    \text{path cost} =
    \text{advertised Rank} + \text{ETX}
\end{equation}

Furthermore, \gls{mrhof} applies a hysteresis mechanism that prevents a node from changing its preferred parent when the improvement in path cost is not sufficiently large.
Therefore, simply advertising a moderately lower Rank does not necessarily cause neighboring nodes to select the malicious node as their parent.

For the first three Sinkhole scenarios, we therefore used an aggressive configuration in which the link-metric contribution was disabled and the malicious node advertised \texttt{ROOT\_RANK}.
This maximized the difference between the malicious route and the currently selected route, making the effect of the attack on the topology clearly observable.

This also explains the behavior observed in the fourth Sinkhole scenario.
Here, the original \gls{mrhof} path-cost calculation and hysteresis mechanism were preserved, while the malicious node used the less aggressive Rank calculation based on \texttt{ROOT\_RANK + min\_hoprankinc}.
When placed at the bottleneck, this configuration did not provide a sufficiently attractive alternative to the existing parent, and the resulting \gls{dag} remained substantially unchanged.
Consequently, the attack had little practical effect in that position.

When the same configuration was instead used with the malicious node placed near a leaf, the \gls{ids} was still able to identify a Rank anomaly.
This shows that the effectiveness and detectability of a Sinkhole attack depend not only on the advertised Rank, but also on the attacker's position and on the parent-selection mechanisms of the routing protocol.

To visualize the effect of the Sinkhole attack on the routing topology, the \glspl{dag} obtained before and after the attack are compared in Fig.~\ref{fig:networks}.

In the scenarios where the \gls{dag} changes, the nodes redirected through the Sinkhole generally become one hop farther from the root, since their new routing path passes through the malicious node. 
This effect is particularly visible in Sinkhole 1, Sinkhole 2, and Sinkhole 4.

In contrast, the \gls{dag} of Sinkhole 3 remains unchanged after the attack. 
Although the Sinkhole is located at a bottleneck position, the existing routing structure and the parent-selection mechanism prevent the advertised Rank from causing a topology change. 
This result further highlights the influence of the node's position on the effectiveness of the attack.

\subsection{IDS effectiveness}
\label{subsec:ids_effectiveness}
The results show that the effectiveness of the developed \gls{ids} depends strongly on the type of anomaly being monitored and on the assumptions made during its design.
The proposed approach is intentionally lightweight and rule-based: it extracts relevant events from the Contiki-NG logs, correlates them according to the expected \gls{rpl} behavior, and generates an alert when a predefined condition is violated.
This follows the general event-processing principles commonly adopted in security monitoring systems, where event collection, correlation, and alerting form the basis for identifying security-relevant activity.

For the Blackhole scenarios, the forwarding-based rule proved effective when the malicious node actually forwarded traffic.
However, it could not identify a Blackhole node that had no children and therefore received no packets to forward.
This highlights an important limitation of deterministic rules: their effectiveness is directly related to the availability of observable evidence.

A similar limitation emerged for the Sinkhole detection.
The Rank-consistency rule successfully identified the manipulated topology when the attack produced an inconsistent parent--child Rank relationship.
However, the additional Rank-change rule proved too sensitive, as Rank transitions occurring during normal \gls{dodag} construction were also reported as suspicious.

These observations suggest that an \gls{ids} should not rely exclusively on isolated events or single threshold-based rules.
Correlating multiple observations, considering their temporal context, and distinguishing normal protocol initialization from anomalous behavior can reduce false positives and improve detection reliability.
This is consistent with the broader distinction between deterministic rule-based detection and more context-aware correlation approaches discussed in previous security-monitoring work \cite{artusi2025siem}.
